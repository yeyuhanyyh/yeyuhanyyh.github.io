An \(\Omega(n^{-1.6342})\) Lower Bound for (Non-Anytime) Gradient Descent

Yuhan Ye1. Based on discussions with Kaizhao Liu1. 1 Massachusetts Institute of Technology. First announced: August 23, 2026. Last updated: August 23, 2026 23:09 (GMT-4).

Introduction

Last week, Ma and Chen proved an \(\Omega(n^{-1.932})\) lower bound for gradient descent (GD) under arbitrary predetermined nonnegative stepsize schedules in smooth convex optimization, and Tsai further improved it to \(\Omega(n^{-\sqrt{3}})\), where \(\sqrt{3}\approx1.7321\) (see his well-written blog post). After multiple rounds of interaction with ChatGPT-5.6 Sol, we obtained the lower bound \(\Omega(n^{-1.6342})\).

In the proof, we use the same hard-function family introduced by Ma and Chen. Tsai's blog post gives an insightful explanation of this construction (cf. Tsai's Lemma 6). Our improvement begins after that construction. We estimate the resulting sequence inequality more precisely through term-by-term control of selected long steps instead of compressing those steps into a harmonic mean (cf. Tsai's Lemma 7).

AI Disclosure. This improvement was developed through multiple rounds of interaction with ChatGPT-5.6 Sol. The generated proof was disorganized and difficult to follow. The author simplified and rewrote the mathematical presentation.

Problem Setup

Let \(\mathcal F_1(\mathbb R^d)\) be the class of convex \(1\)-smooth functions on \(\mathbb R^d\) whose sets of minimizers are nonempty. Given an integer iteration horizon \(n\ge1\), an initial point \(x_1\in\mathbb R^d\), and a predetermined schedule \(H=(h_1,\ldots,h_n)\in(0,\infty)^n\), GD generates \[ x_{k+1}=x_k-h_k\nabla f(x_k),\qquad 1\le k\le n. \] The schedule may depend on \(n\), but not on \(f\).

Define the worst-case convergence rate by \[ R_n(H):= \sup_{d\in\mathbb N} \sup_{f\in\mathcal F_1(\mathbb R^d)} \sup_{x^\star\in\arg\min f} \sup_{x_1\in\mathbb R^d\setminus\{x^\star\}} \frac{f(x_{n+1})-f(x^\star)} {\frac12\lVert x_1-x^\star\rVert^2}. \]

Our target. Improve Tsai's lower bound \(R_n(H)=\Omega(n^{-\sqrt{3}})\) and bring its exponent as close as possible to the \(O(n^{-\log_2(1+\sqrt{2})})\) upper bound of Altschuler and Parrilo.

Main Results

Theorem 1. (Tsai) There exists a universal constant \(c>0\) such that, for every integer \(n\ge1\) and every \(H\in(0,\infty)^n\), \(R_n(H)\ge c n^{-\sqrt{3}}\).

Our sharper analysis gives the following result.

Theorem 2 (Main result). There exists a universal constant \(c>0\) such that, for every integer \(n\ge1\) and every \(H\in(0,\infty)^n\), \(R_n(H)\ge c n^{-1.6342}\).

The first part of our proof follows Ma and Chen and Tsai exactly. The same schedule-dependent hard functions reduce the optimization problem to Lemma 2 below. For the construction itself, see Tsai's insightful explanation. We do not repeat it here. Using the same notation, write \(h_{a:b}:=\sum_{k=a}^b h_k\), with an empty sum equal to zero. For an integer \(1\le m\le n\), choose \[ 0<t_1<\cdots<t_m\le n, \qquad h_{t_i}>1, \] set \(t_0=0\), \(t_{m+1}=n+1\), and define \[ S_i:=h_{t_{i-1}+1:t_i-1}, \qquad 1\le i\le m+1. \]

Lemma 2. (cf. Ma and Chen, Theorem 4.1 and Tsai, Lemma 6). For every such selection,

\[ R_n(H)\ge \frac{1}{S_m+h_{t_m}+2S_{m+1}+1} \prod_{i=1}^{m-1} \frac{S_{i+1}+h_{t_{i+1}}} {S_i+h_{t_i}+S_{i+1}+h_{t_{i+1}}} \prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{h_{t_i}-1}{S_i+1}. \tag{1} \]

Lemma 2 completes the hard-function construction. From this point on, the rest of the proof amounts to a sequence inequality for the right-hand side of (1). Tsai treats the selected long steps collectively through a harmonic mean (cf. Tsai's Lemma 7) and a counting function (cf. Tsai's Lemma 8), which loses information about their individual sizes and chronological pairings. To obtain a sharper estimate, we retain every factor coupling two consecutive selected steps (Lemma 3) and apply sharper term-by-term estimates (Lemma 4 and Lemma 6).

The equivalent sequence problem

Lemma 3 (Clean sequence problem). Fix \(\alpha>0\), so the target rate exponent is \(1+\alpha\). For \(w,z,x,y>0\), define

\[ K_{w,z}(x,y):= \frac{\sqrt{xy}\,(w+z+x+y)}{\sqrt{wz(w+x)(z+y)}} \tag{2} . \]

To prove \(R_n(H)\ge c_\alpha(n+1)^{-(1+\alpha)}\) for every \(n\) and \(H\), it is enough to find a constant \(C_\alpha<\infty\), depending only on \(\alpha\), such that the following holds for every integer \(q\ge2\). Given positive sequences \(x_1,\ldots,x_q\) and \(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_q\), write \(\omega_1^\downarrow\ge\cdots\ge\omega_q^\downarrow\) for the decreasing rearrangement of \((\omega_i)\). If

\[ \sum_{i=1}^q x_i<q, \qquad \sum_{s=m+1}^q \omega_s^\downarrow \ge q\left[\left(\frac qm\right)^\alpha-1\right], \qquad 1\le m<q. \tag{3} \]

then

\[ E_{\mathrm{end}} \prod_{i=1}^{q-1}K_{\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}}(x_i,x_{i+1}) \le C_\alpha, \tag{4} \]

where

\[ E_{\mathrm{end}}:= \sqrt{x_1x_q}\sqrt{\frac{1+x_1/\omega_1}{1+x_q/\omega_q}} \left[1+\frac{x_q+2\left(q-\sum_{i=1}^q x_i\right)}{\omega_q}\right]. \tag{5} \]
Proof sketch

For a fixed schedule \(H\), let \(a_1\ge\cdots\ge a_r>0\) be the decreasing rearrangement of its positive excesses \((h_k-1)_+\). Define \[ B:=1+\sum_{k=1}^n\min\{h_k,1\}, \qquad D_s:=B+\sum_{\ell=s+1}^r a_\ell, \qquad 1\le s\le r. \]

Set \(\delta:=(1+2\sum_{k=1}^n h_k)^{-1}\) and consider the one-dimensional convex, \(1\)-smooth Huber function \[ f_\delta(x):= \begin{cases} x^2/2,& |x|\le\delta,\\ \delta|x|-\delta^2/2,& |x|>\delta. \end{cases} \] Starting from \(\xi_1=1\), an induction gives \(\xi_k=1-\delta\sum_{j<k}h_j>\delta\). Hence every iterate stays on the positive affine branch, and the ratio in the definition of \(R_n(H)\) is \(2f_\delta(\xi_{n+1})=\delta\). If \(r=0\), then \(\delta=(2B-1)^{-1}\), so \[ R_n(H)\ge\frac1{2B-1}\ge\frac1{2B}. \]

Assume \(r\ge1\), and choose \[ q\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{1\le s\le r}D_s s^\alpha. \] Thus \[ D_q q^\alpha\le D_s s^\alpha, \qquad 1\le s\le r. \] If \(q=1\), let \(a_1\) be the largest excess. Since \(D_1+a_1=1+\sum_{k=1}^n h_k\), the Huber construction gives \(R_n(H)\ge[2(D_1+a_1)-1]^{-1}\), which is at least \(1/(4D_1)\) when \(a_1\le D_1\). If \(a_1>D_1\), choose an index \(t\) with \(h_t-1=a_1\) and apply Lemma 2 with \(m=1\) and \(t_1=t\). Write \(U_1:=S_1+1\) and \(V:=S_2+1\). Then \(D_1=U_1+V\), and Lemma 2 gives \[ R_n(H)\ge \frac{a_1}{U_1(U_1+a_1+2V-1)} >\frac1{3D_1} >\frac1{4D_1}. \] Since \(U_1\le D_1\) and \(U_1+a_1+2V-1<a_1+2D_1<3a_1\), both cases give \(R_n(H)\ge1/(4D_1)\). Taking \(s=r\) in \(D_q q^\alpha\le D_s s^\alpha\), with \(q=1\), gives \(D_1\le D_r r^\alpha=Br^\alpha\), and hence \[ R_n(H)\ge\frac1{4B(r+1)^\alpha}. \]

Now assume \(q\ge2\). Let \(t_1<\cdots<t_q\) be the indices of the \(q\) largest positive excesses, listed in increasing order, and set \(\widetilde a_i:=h_{t_i}-1\). Thus \((\widetilde a_1,\ldots,\widetilde a_q)\) consists of \(a_1,\ldots,a_q\), listed in the order in which the corresponding steps occur. With \(S_i\) as in Lemma 2, define \[ U_i:=S_i+1\quad(1\le i\le q), \qquad V:=S_{q+1}+1, \qquad \bar U:=\frac{D_q}{q}. \] The gap sums \(S_1,\ldots,S_{q+1}\) contain exactly the unselected steps. Hence, by the definition of \(B\), \[ D_q=\sum_{i=1}^q U_i+V. \] Set \[ x_i:=\frac{U_i}{\bar U}, \qquad \omega_i:=\frac{\widetilde a_i}{\bar U}, \qquad 1\le i\le q. \] Then \(\sum_i x_i=q-V/\bar U<q\), while the decreasing rearrangement satisfies \(\omega_s^\downarrow=a_s/\bar U\). For every \(1\le m<q\), the comparison \(D_q q^\alpha\le D_m m^\alpha\) gives \[ 1+\frac1q\sum_{s=m+1}^q\omega_s^\downarrow =\frac{D_m}{D_q} \ge\left(\frac qm\right)^\alpha, \] which is exactly (3).

Let \(G_q\) be the expression on the right-hand side of (1), with \(m=q\) and the indices \(t_1,\ldots,t_q\) chosen above. Lemma 2 gives \(R_n(H)\ge G_q\). Rewriting the factors in (1) in terms of \(x_i\), \(\omega_i\), and \(\bar U\) gives \[ G_q^{-1} =\bar U\,\widehat E_{\mathrm{end}} \prod_{i=1}^{q-1}K_{\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}}(x_i,x_{i+1}), \] where \[ \widehat E_{\mathrm{end}} =\sqrt{x_1x_q} \sqrt{\frac{1+x_1/\omega_1}{1+x_q/\omega_q}} \left[1+\frac{x_q+2(q-\sum_i x_i)-\bar U^{-1}}{\omega_q}\right] \le E_{\mathrm{end}}. \] Using (4) and \(\widehat E_{\mathrm{end}}\le E_{\mathrm{end}}\), we obtain \(G_q\ge q/(C_\alpha D_q)\). Taking \(s=r\) in \(D_q q^\alpha\le D_s s^\alpha\) gives \[ D_q q^\alpha\le D_r r^\alpha=Br^\alpha. \] Therefore \[ R_n(H) \ge\frac{q}{C_\alpha D_q} \ge\frac{q^{1+\alpha}}{C_\alpha Br^\alpha} \ge\frac1{C_\alpha B(r+1)^\alpha}. \]

Since \(B\le n+1\) and \(r+1\le n+1\), combining the cases \(r=0\), \(q=1\), and \(q\ge2\) yields \[ R_n(H)\ge c_\alpha(n+1)^{-(1+\alpha)}, \qquad c_\alpha:=\min\{C_\alpha^{-1},1/4\}. \]

Eliminating the variables \(x_i\)

To make (4) more tractable, we first eliminate the variables \(x_i\). For a parameter \(\lambda>0\), to be chosen later, define \[ \Gamma_\lambda(w,z) := \sup_{x,y>0} \left\{ \log K_{w,z}(x,y)-\lambda(x+y) \right\}. \tag{6} \] By definition, this gives an upper bound for each edge kernel. \[ K_{w,z}(x,y) \le \exp\!\left(\lambda(x+y)+\Gamma_\lambda(w,z)\right). \] Multiplying these inequalities introduces \(\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}(x_i+x_{i+1})\) in the exponent. Condition (3) gives \(\sum_{i=1}^q x_i<q\).

Lemma 4. Fix \(\alpha,\lambda>0\). There is a constant \(C_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty\) such that, for every integer \(q\ge2\), every pair of positive sequences \((x_i)_{i=1}^q\) and \((\omega_i)_{i=1}^q\) satisfying (3) obeys

\[ E_{\mathrm{end}} \prod_{i=1}^{q-1}K_{\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}}(x_i,x_{i+1}) \le C_{\alpha,\lambda} \exp\!\left\{ 2\lambda q+ \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}) \right\}. \tag{7} \]
Proof sketch

Applying the definition of \(\Gamma_\lambda\) to every edge gives the exponential of \[ \lambda\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}(x_i+x_{i+1}) + \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}). \] Since \[ \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}(x_i+x_{i+1}) = 2\sum_{i=1}^q x_i-x_1-x_q, \] the remaining factor is \[ E_{\mathrm{end}} e^{-\lambda\left( 2(q-\sum_i x_i)+x_1+x_q \right)}. \] Taking \(m=q-1\) in (3) gives \(\min_i\omega_i=\omega_q^\downarrow \ge q[(q/(q-1))^\alpha-1] \ge q\alpha\log(q/(q-1))\ge\alpha\). Hence \(\omega_1,\omega_q\ge\alpha\). The last display is therefore a polynomial-growth function of the three nonnegative variables \(x_1,x_q,q-\sum_i x_i\), multiplied by exponential decay. Its supremum is a finite constant \(C_{\alpha,\lambda}\), which proves (7).

Basic properties of \(\Gamma_\lambda\)

Lemma 5. For every \(\lambda>0\), \(\Gamma_\lambda\) has the following properties.

(1) Smoothness. For every \(w,z>0\), the supremum in (6) is attained at a unique pair \((x_\lambda(w,z),y_\lambda(w,z))\in(0,\infty)^2\). The optimizer map \((w,z)\mapsto(x_\lambda(w,z),y_\lambda(w,z))\) and \(\Gamma_\lambda\) are \(C^\infty\) on \((0,\infty)^2\).

(2) Symmetry. \(\Gamma_\lambda(w,z)=\Gamma_\lambda(z,w)\).

(3) Joint convexity. The map \((w,z)\mapsto\Gamma_\lambda(w,z)\) is jointly convex on \((0,\infty)^2\).

(4) Decreasing in each coordinate. \(\Gamma_\lambda\) is strictly decreasing in each coordinate.

(5) Strict submodularity. \[ \partial_{wz}^2\Gamma_\lambda(w,z)<0. \tag{8} \]

Proof sketch

(1) Smoothness. Let \[ F_{w,z}(x,y):=\log K_{w,z}(x,y)-\lambda(x+y). \] For fixed \(w,z>0\), \(F_{w,z}(x,y)\to-\infty\) as \(x\downarrow0\), \(y\downarrow0\), or \(x+y\to\infty\). Hence the supremum in (6) is attained at an interior point. Write \[ A:=w+x,\qquad B:=z+y,\qquad \Sigma:=A+B. \] For every nonzero direction \((a,b)\), \[ D^2_{x,y}F_{w,z}[(a,b)]^2 =-\frac{a^2}{2x^2}-\frac{b^2}{2y^2} -\frac{(a+b)^2}{\Sigma^2} +\frac{a^2}{2A^2}+\frac{b^2}{2B^2}<0. \] Thus the maximizer is unique. At the maximizer, the first-order equations imply \[ x\left(1+\frac{x}{w}\right) =y\left(1+\frac{y}{z}\right)=:\tau. \] For \(u>0\), define \[ X_u(\tau):=\frac{\sqrt{u^2+4u\tau}-u}{2}, \qquad A_u(\tau):=u+X_u(\tau). \] Then \(x=X_w(\tau)\), \(y=X_z(\tau)\), and \(\tau\) is the unique solution of \[ \frac1{2\tau}+\frac1{A_w(\tau)+A_z(\tau)}=\lambda. \] The left-hand side decreases from \(+\infty\) to \(0\), and \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\tau} \left[ \frac1{2\tau}+\frac1{A_w(\tau)+A_z(\tau)} \right] =-\frac1{2\tau^2} -\frac{A_w'(\tau)+A_z'(\tau)} {(A_w(\tau)+A_z(\tau))^2}<0. \] By the implicit function theorem and uniqueness of the root, \(\tau(w,z)\), and hence the unique optimizer, is \(C^\infty\) throughout \((0,\infty)^2\). Since \(\Gamma_\lambda\) is the objective evaluated at this optimizer, it is also \(C^\infty\). At the optimizer, \(A=A_w(\tau)\) and \(B=A_z(\tau)\).

(2) Symmetry. The definition (6) is invariant under \((w,x)\leftrightarrow(z,y)\), which proves Property (2).

(3) Joint convexity. For fixed \(x,y\), the Hessian in \((w,z)\) has quadratic form \[ \frac{a^2}{2w^2}+\frac{a^2}{2A^2} +\frac{b^2}{2z^2}+\frac{b^2}{2B^2} -\frac{(a+b)^2}{(A+B)^2}\ge0. \] The first four terms dominate \(a^2/A^2+b^2/B^2\), and Cauchy–Schwarz gives \[ \frac{a^2}{A^2}+\frac{b^2}{B^2} \ge \frac{(a+b)^2}{(A+B)^2}. \] Taking the supremum in (6) preserves convexity, proving Property (3).

(4) Decreasing in each coordinate. At the unique optimizer, Danskin's theorem gives \[ \partial_w\Gamma_\lambda = \frac1\Sigma-\frac1{2w}-\frac1{2A}. \] Equivalently, \[ -\partial_w\Gamma_\lambda =\frac{x}{2wA}+\frac{B}{A\Sigma}>0, \] and similarly in \(z\). This proves Property (4).

(5) Strict submodularity. Define \[ g(\tau;w,z):= \frac1{2\tau}+\frac1{A_w(\tau)+A_z(\tau)}-\lambda. \] At fixed \(w\), \(\partial_\tau g<0\), while \(\partial_z g<0\) because \(A_z(\tau)\) increases with \(z\). Thus \(\partial_z\tau=-(\partial_zg)/(\partial_\tau g)<0\). At the optimizer, \(\Sigma=(\lambda-1/(2\tau))^{-1}\) and \(A=A_w(\tau)\). Since \(\Sigma\) decreases with \(\tau\) and \(A_w'(\tau)>0\), \[ \partial_z\Sigma>0,\qquad \partial_z A=A_w'(\tau)\partial_z\tau<0. \] Differentiating the formula for \(\partial_w\Gamma_\lambda\) gives \[ \partial_{wz}^2\Gamma_\lambda = -\frac{\partial_z\Sigma}{\Sigma^2} +\frac{\partial_z A}{2A^2}<0, \] proving Property (5).

Upper bounding \(\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1})\)

Recall from Lemma 3 that \(\omega_1^\downarrow\ge\cdots\ge\omega_q^\downarrow\) is the decreasing rearrangement of \(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_q\).

For a positive vector \(v=(v_1,\ldots,v_N)\) and \(0\le k\le\lfloor N/2\rfloor\), let \(\mathcal M_k(N)\) be the set of all collections of \(k\) disjoint unordered pairs from \(\{1,\ldots,N\}\). Define \[ \Phi_{k,\lambda}(v_1,\ldots,v_N) := \max_{M\in\mathcal M_k(N)} \sum_{\{a,b\}\in M}\Gamma_\lambda(v_a,v_b). \] Thus \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}(v)\) is the largest value of a \(k\)-edge matching formed from the entries of \(v\). We use the convention \(\Phi_{0,\lambda}=0\).

The odd and even edges of the chronological path form two matchings.

Set \(k_q:=\lfloor(q-1)/2\rfloor\). By the coordinatewise-decreasing property in Lemma 5(4), \(\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_j)\le\Gamma_\lambda(\alpha,\alpha)\), since (3) with \(m=q-1\) gives \(\omega_i,\omega_j\ge\alpha\). Considering odd and even \(q\) separately gives \[ \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}) \le 2\Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\omega_q^\downarrow,\ldots,\omega_2^\downarrow) +C_{\alpha,\lambda}. \tag{9} \] See the proof sketch for details.

We now bound \(\Phi_{k_q,\lambda}\) through two finer reductions, using the properties of \(\Gamma_\lambda\) established in Lemma 5.

Step 1. Majorization: Replace \((\omega_q^\downarrow,\ldots,\omega_2^\downarrow)\) by \((\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)})\)

For \(2\le s\le q\), define the explicit comparison weights

\[ \bar w_s^{(q)} := q^{1+\alpha}\bigl((s-1)^{-\alpha}-s^{-\alpha}\bigr), \qquad 2\le s\le q. \tag{10} \]

Their tails satisfy \[ \sum_{s=m+1}^q\bar w_s^{(q)} =q\left[\left(\frac qm\right)^\alpha-1\right]. \] Together with (3), this gives \[ \sum_{s=m+1}^q\omega_s^\downarrow \ge \sum_{s=m+1}^q\bar w_s^{(q)}, \qquad 1\le m<q. \] Because \(\Gamma_\lambda\) is symmetric, jointly convex, and coordinatewise decreasing (Lemma 5), the maximum-matching functional \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}\) is symmetric, convex, and coordinatewise decreasing as well. The tail comparison above therefore gives \[ \Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\omega_q^\downarrow,\ldots,\omega_2^\downarrow) \le \Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)}). \]

Step 2. Reduce to the worst pairing

Let \(0<v_1\le\cdots\le v_N\) and \(0\le k\le\lfloor N/2\rfloor\). Since \(\Gamma_\lambda\) decreases in each coordinate, a maximizing matching in \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}(v)\) may be chosen to use the \(2k\) smallest entries. Symmetry and strict submodularity then pair the two ends of this ordered list. \[ \Phi_{k,\lambda}(v_1,\ldots,v_N) = \sum_{j=1}^k \Gamma_\lambda(v_j,v_{2k+1-j}). \tag{11} \] The numbers in (10) satisfy \(\bar w_q^{(q)}\le\cdots\le\bar w_2^{(q)}\), so (11) applies directly to \((\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)})\). In particular,

\[ \Phi_{k_q,\lambda}(\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)}) =\sum_{j=1}^{k_q} \Gamma_\lambda(\bar w_{q+1-j}^{(q)},\bar w_{q-2k_q+j}^{(q)}). \]

It remains to estimate this explicit sum.

By the mean value theorem, for every \(2\le s\le q\), there is \(\xi_s\in(s-1,s)\) such that

\[ \bar w_s^{(q)}=W_\alpha(\xi_s/q), \qquad W_\alpha(t):=\alpha t^{-1-\alpha},\quad 0<t\le1. \tag{12} \]

Every integral below uses the continuous extension at \(t=0\) established in the proof of Lemma 6. Using (12), the paired sum in (11) is a Riemann sum for the integral below. Controlling the endpoint and rounding errors uniformly in \(q\) gives

\[ \Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)}) \le q\int_0^{1/2} \Gamma_\lambda\bigl(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(1-t)\bigr)\,dt +C_{\alpha,\lambda}. \]

Combining this estimate with (9) gives the following lemma.

Lemma 6. Fix \(\alpha,\lambda>0\). There is a constant \(C_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty\) such that, for every integer \(q\ge2\) and every positive sequence \(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_q\) satisfying the tail condition in (3),

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}) \le 2q\int_0^{1/2} \Gamma_\lambda\bigl(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(1-t)\bigr)\,dt +C_{\alpha,\lambda}. \tag{13} \]
Proof sketch

(1) Split the consecutive pairs by parity. Put \(k_q=\lfloor(q-1)/2\rfloor\), and choose one occurrence of \(\omega_1^\downarrow\). If \(q\) is odd, each parity matching has \(k_q\) pairs and leaves one index unused. If a matching contains the chosen occurrence, replace it by the unused index. Its value is no larger, so Lemma 5(4) shows that this replacement cannot decrease the sum.

If \(q\) is even, one parity matching has \(k_q\) pairs and is handled in the same way. The other matching is perfect. Delete its unique pair containing the chosen occurrence. Each remaining matching now has \(k_q\) pairs drawn from \((\omega_q^\downarrow,\ldots,\omega_2^\downarrow)\), and is therefore bounded by \(\Phi_{k_q,\lambda}(\omega_q^\downarrow,\ldots,\omega_2^\downarrow)\). Taking \(m=q-1\) in (3) gives \[ \omega_q^\downarrow \ge q\left[\left(\frac q{q-1}\right)^\alpha-1\right] \ge q\alpha\log\frac q{q-1}\ge\alpha, \] where the last step uses \(\log(1+t)\ge t/(1+t)\). Hence every \(\omega_i\ge\alpha\), and Lemma 5(4) gives \(\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_j)\le \Gamma_\lambda(\alpha,\alpha)\). In particular, the pair deleted in the even case is bounded by \(\max\{0,\Gamma_\lambda(\alpha,\alpha)\}\). This proves (9).

(2) Compare the two ordered vectors. Each fixed-matching sum in the definition of \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}\) is convex and coordinatewise decreasing. Taking the maximum over all matchings preserves these properties. The collection of matchings is invariant under coordinate permutations, so \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}\) is also symmetric.

Let \(a_1\le\cdots\le a_N\) and \(b_1\le\cdots\le b_N\) satisfy \(\sum_{i=1}^\ell a_i\ge\sum_{i=1}^\ell b_i\) for every \(\ell\). Choose an ordered subgradient \(g\in\partial\Phi_{k,\lambda}(a)\) with \(g_1\le\cdots\le g_N\le0\). Symmetry and convexity give the ordering, while coordinatewise decrease gives the signs. Put \(D_\ell:=\sum_{i=1}^\ell(b_i-a_i)\le0\). Summation by parts yields \[ g\cdot(b-a) =g_ND_N+\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}(g_\ell-g_{\ell+1})D_\ell\ge0. \] The subgradient inequality therefore gives \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}(a)\le\Phi_{k,\lambda}(b)\).

The comparison weights satisfy \[ \bar w_s^{(q)} =q^{1+\alpha}\int_{s-1}^{s}\alpha u^{-1-\alpha}\,du. \] Since the integrand decreases, this proves \(\bar w_q^{(q)}\le\cdots\le\bar w_2^{(q)}\). Their tail sums telescope to the right-hand side of (3). Apply the preceding partial-sum argument with \[ a=(\omega_q^\downarrow,\ldots,\omega_2^\downarrow), \qquad b=(\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)}). \] This proves the comparison in Step 1.

(3) Determine the maximizing pairing. For \(0<a\le b\le c\le d\), symmetry and strict submodularity give \[ \Gamma_\lambda(a,d)+\Gamma_\lambda(b,c) \ge\Gamma_\lambda(a,c)+\Gamma_\lambda(b,d), \] and \[ \Gamma_\lambda(a,d)+\Gamma_\lambda(b,c) \ge\Gamma_\lambda(a,b)+\Gamma_\lambda(c,d). \] Coordinatewise decrease selects the \(2k\) smallest coordinates, and repeated applications of these two exchange inequalities produce the pairing in (11).

(4) Control the endpoint \(t=0\). Write \(q=2\ell+1\) or \(q=2\ell\). The paired sum produced by (11) becomes, respectively, \[ \sum_{j=1}^{\ell} \Gamma_\lambda\!\left(\bar w_{j+1}^{(q)},\bar w_{q+1-j}^{(q)}\right), \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1} \Gamma_\lambda\!\left(\bar w_{j+2}^{(q)},\bar w_{q+1-j}^{(q)}\right). \] By the mean value theorem, for each \(2\le s\le q\) there is \(\xi_s\in(s-1,s)\) such that \[ \bar w_s^{(q)}=W_\alpha(\xi_s/q). \] We next compare these sums with a Riemann sum along \((t,1-t)\). To control the terms near \(t=0\), define \(G(t,s):=\Gamma_\lambda(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(s))\). Let \(w=W_\alpha(t)\), \(z=W_\alpha(s)\), and let \((x,y)\) be the optimizer in (6). Set \(A_w=w+x\), \(A_z=z+y\), and \(\Sigma=A_w+A_z\). The derivative formula in Lemma 5 gives the exact identity \[ -\partial_w\Gamma_\lambda =\frac1{2w}+\frac1{2A_w}-\frac1\Sigma =\frac{x}{2wA_w}+\frac{A_z}{A_w\Sigma}. \] For \(s\in[1/2,1]\), the value \(z\) stays in a compact subinterval of \((0,\infty)\). The optimizer equation derived in the proof of Lemma 5 and \(A_z(\tau)\ge\sqrt{z\tau}\) give \[ \lambda \le \frac1{2\tau}+\frac1{\sqrt{z\tau}}, \] so \(\tau=O_{\alpha,\lambda}(1)\). Since \(x,y\le\tau\), both \(x\) and \(A_z=z+y\) are uniformly bounded. As \(A_w,\Sigma\ge w\), the preceding identity proves \[ -\partial_w\Gamma_\lambda=O_{\alpha,\lambda}(w^{-2}). \] Moreover \(|W_\alpha'(t)|W_\alpha(t)^{-2}=O_\alpha(t^\alpha)\). The derivative of \(G\) in its second coordinate is uniformly bounded because \(W_\alpha(s)\) and \(W_\alpha'(s)\) remain in compact ranges. Thus \(G\) has a bounded Lipschitz extension on \([0,1/2]\times[1/2,1]\). The constants are locally uniform when \(\alpha\) ranges over a compact subset of \((0,\infty)\).

(5) Compare the finite sums with the integral. When the displayed sum is nonempty, remove its final term. In both parity cases this term is \(\Gamma_\lambda(\bar w_{\ell+1}^{(q)},\bar w_{\ell+2}^{(q)})\). Formula (12) places both arguments in a fixed compact interval depending only on \(\alpha\), so its contribution is bounded independently of \(q\). The remaining terms form Riemann sums for the same integral. The Lipschitz bound above controls the rounding error in each term, and the missing endpoint intervals contribute only a bounded amount. Consequently, for either parity, \[ \Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)}) =q\int_0^{1/2} \Gamma_\lambda\bigl(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(1-t)\bigr)\,dt +O_{\alpha,\lambda}(1). \] When \(q=2\), \(\Phi_{k_q,\lambda}=\Phi_{0,\lambda}=0\). Since the integral is finite, the same estimate holds after enlarging the \(O_{\alpha,\lambda}(1)\) term, and (9) also bounds the single pair in the original sum. Combining the estimate above with (9) proves (13).

The remaining task is a scalar inequality. Define

\[ J(\alpha,\lambda) := 2\lambda+ 2\int_0^{1/2} \Gamma_\lambda\bigl(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(1-t)\bigr)\,dt. \tag{14} \]

Combining Lemmas 4 and 6 gives

\[ E_{\mathrm{end}} \prod_{i=1}^{q-1}K_{\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}}(x_i,x_{i+1}) \le C_{\alpha,\lambda}e^{qJ(\alpha,\lambda)}. \tag{15} \]

Therefore, if \(J(\alpha,\lambda)\le0\), then (4) holds uniformly in \(q\), and Lemma 3 gives \[ R_n(H)\ge c_{\alpha,\lambda}(n+1)^{-(1+\alpha)}. \tag{16} \] Thus we seek a small \(\alpha>0\) and some \(\lambda>0\) for which \(J(\alpha,\lambda)\le0\).

Take \(\alpha=0.6342\) and \(\lambda=0.4506\). A validated interval-arithmetic computation (verification appendix) certifies \[ J(0.6342,0.4506)<-0.00005<0. \] Since \(n+1\le2n\) for \(n\ge1\), the fixed factor \(2^{1.6342}\) is absorbed into the constant. Hence \(R_n(H)=\Omega(n^{-1.6342})\), proving Theorem 2.

Let \(\alpha_0\) be the unique zero of \(J(\alpha,0.4506)\), and define \(p_0:=1+\alpha_0\). Numerically, \(p_0\approx1.6341605846\).

Proof sketch

When \(J(\alpha,\lambda)\le0\), equation (15) supplies the uniform constant required in (4). Lemma 3 then gives (16).

For every fixed \(\lambda>0\), the map \(\alpha\mapsto J(\alpha,\lambda)\) is continuous and strictly decreasing. Indeed \(W_\alpha(t)\) increases with \(\alpha\), while \(\Gamma_\lambda\) decreases in each coordinate. The endpoint estimates in the proof of Lemma 6 give locally uniform domination.

As \(\alpha\downarrow0\), both profile coordinates tend to zero uniformly on, for example, \([1/4,3/4]\). Evaluating the supremum defining \(\Gamma_\lambda\) at any fixed \(x,y>0\) shows that the integrand in (14) tends uniformly to \(+\infty\) on \([1/4,1/2]\). Monotonicity supplies an integrable lower bound on the remaining part of the interval, so this divergence cannot be cancelled. Hence \[ \lim_{\alpha\downarrow0}J(\alpha,\lambda)=+\infty. \]

As \(\alpha\to\infty\), both profile coordinates are at least \(\alpha\). At the optimizer, \[ \Gamma_\lambda(w,z) \le \log\frac{x+y}{\alpha}-\lambda(x+y) \le-\log(\alpha\lambda)-1, \] uniformly in \(t\). Therefore \(\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}J(\alpha,\lambda)=-\infty\). Thus \(J(\,\cdot\,,0.4506)\) has a unique zero \(\alpha_0\).

Computer-assisted verification. For the exact decimal parameters \(\alpha=0.6342\) and \(\lambda=0.4506\), the validated computation partitions \([0,1/2]\) into \(2^{16}\) rational cells. On every cell it encloses the unique optimizing \(\tau\) by sign-verified brackets and evaluates \(\Gamma_\lambda\) using directed outward rounding. Coordinatewise monotonicity controls the cell adjacent to \(t=0\). Summing the resulting interval bounds certifies \(J(0.6342,0.4506)<-0.00005\). High-precision quadrature gives the numerical value of \(p_0\) displayed above.

Concluding Remarks

Starting from the same hard-function construction and the corresponding chain bound in Lemma 2 (cf. Tsai's Lemma 6), we use a finer term-by-term analysis of the full chronological sequence. This improves Tsai's lower bound from \(\Omega(n^{-\sqrt{3}})\) to \(\Omega(n^{-1.6342})\).

At the exponential scale relevant to the final rate, our sequence estimates appear fairly tight. One plausible source of slack is the parity split in (9). The odd and even edges form two alternating matchings of the same chronological path, but we bound them independently by the same unconstrained worst-matching value. For strictly ordered weights, the two matchings need not simultaneously attain the outside-in maximizer in (11).

A more structural question is whether Lemma 2 can already yield the \(\Omega(n^{-1.271553\ldots})\) lower bound, matching the silver-stepsize upper bound. Our refined analysis makes us skeptical that this route will close the whole gap, but it does not give an impossibility result. It may instead be necessary to construct hard functions with a hierarchy of scales, so that long steps at different scales interact within the same instance, mirroring the recursive structure of silver stepsizes.
