An \(\Omega(n^{-1.6342})\) Lower Bound for (Non-Anytime) Gradient Descent

Yuhan Ye1. Based on discussions with Kaizhao Liu1. 1 Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Last update: August 23, 2026 10:24 (GMT-4).

Introduction

Last week, Ma and Chen proved an \(\Omega(n^{-1.932})\) lower bound for gradient descent (GD) under arbitrary non-adaptive stepsize schedules in smooth convex optimization, and Tsai further improved it to \(\Omega(n^{-\sqrt3})\approx\Omega(n^{-1.7321})\) (see his well-written blog post). After multiple rounds of interaction with ChatGPT-5.6 Sol, we obtained the lower bound \(\Omega(n^{-1.6342})\).

In the proof, we use the same hard-function family introduced by Ma and Chen. Tsai's blog post gives a beautiful geometric explanation of this construction. Our improvement begins after that construction. We estimate the resulting sequence inequality more precisely through term-by-term control, rearrangement, and rescaling of the full chronological sequence of selected long steps instead of compressing those steps into a harmonic mean (cf. Tsai's Lemma 7).

AI Disclosure. This improvement was developed through multiple rounds of interaction with ChatGPT-5.6 Sol. The author directed the mathematical exploration and rewrote the presentation.

Problem Setup

Let \(\mathcal F_1(\mathbb R^d)\) be the class of convex \(1\)-smooth functions on \(\mathbb R^d\) whose sets of minimizers are nonempty. Given a stopping time \(n\in\mathbb N\), an initial point \(x_1\in\mathbb R^d\), and a predetermined schedule \(H=(h_1,\ldots,h_n)\in(0,\infty)^n\), GD generates \[ x_{k+1}=x_k-h_k\nabla f(x_k),\qquad k\in[n]. \] The schedule may depend on \(n\), but not on \(f\).

Define the worst-case convergence rate by \[ R_n(H):= \sup_{d\in\mathbb N} \sup_{f\in\mathcal F_1(\mathbb R^d)} \sup_{x^\star\in\arg\min f} \sup_{x_1\in\mathbb R^d} \frac{f(x_{n+1})-f(x^\star)} {\frac12\lVert x_1-x^\star\rVert^2}. \]

Our target. Improve Tsai's lower bound \(R_n(H)=\Omega(n^{-\sqrt3})\) and bring its exponent as close as possible to the \(O(n^{-\log_2(1+\sqrt2)})\) upper bound of Altschuler and Parrilo.

Main Results

Theorem 1. (Tsai) There exists a universal constant \(c_{\mathrm T}>0\) such that, for every \(n\in\mathbb N\) and every \(H\in(0,\infty)^n\), \(R_n(H)\ge c_{\mathrm T}n^{-\sqrt3}\).

Our sharper analysis gives the following result.

Theorem 2 (Main result). There exists a universal constant \(c>0\) such that, for every \(n\in\mathbb N\) and every \(H\in(0,\infty)^n\), \(R_n(H)\ge c n^{-1.6342}\).

The first part of our proof follows Ma and Chen and Tsai exactly. The same schedule-dependent hard functions reduce the optimization problem to Lemma 2 below. For the construction itself, see Tsai's beautiful explanation. We do not repeat it here. Using the same notation, write \(h_{a:b}:=\sum_{k=a}^b h_k\), with an empty sum equal to zero. Choose \[ 0<t_1<\cdots<t_m\le n, \qquad h_{t_i}>1, \] set \(t_0=0\), \(t_{m+1}=n+1\), and define \[ S_i:=h_{t_{i-1}+1:t_i-1}, \qquad i\in[m+1]. \]

Lemma 2. (cf. Ma and Chen, Theorem 4.1 and Tsai, Lemma 6.) For every such selection,

\[ R_n(H)\ge \frac{1}{S_m+h_{t_m}+2S_{m+1}+1} \prod_{i=1}^{m-1} \frac{S_{i+1}+h_{t_{i+1}}} {S_i+h_{t_i}+S_{i+1}+h_{t_{i+1}}} \prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{h_{t_i}-1}{S_i+1}. \tag{1} \]

Lemma 2 completes the hard-function construction. From this point on, the proof is a sequence inequality for its right-hand side. Tsai treats the selected long steps collectively through a harmonic mean (cf. Tsai's Lemma 7) and a counting function (cf. Tsai's Lemma 8), which loses information about their individual sizes and chronological pairings. To obtain a sharper estimate, we retain every factor coupling two consecutive selected steps (Lemma 3) and apply sharper termwise estimates, rearrangements, and rescalings (Lemma 4, Lemma 5, and Lemma 6).

The equivalent sequence problem

Lemma 3 (Clean sequence problem). Fix \(\alpha>0\), so the target rate exponent is \(1+\alpha\). For \(w,z,x,y>0\), define

\[ K_{w,z}(x,y):= \frac{\sqrt{xy}\,(w+z+x+y)}{\sqrt{wz(w+x)(z+y)}} \tag{2} \]

To prove \(R_n(H)\ge c_\alpha(n+1)^{-(1+\alpha)}\) for every \(n\) and \(H\), it is enough to find a constant \(C_\alpha<\infty\), depending only on \(\alpha\), such that the following holds for every integer \(q\ge2\). Given positive sequences \(x_1,\ldots,x_q\) and \(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_q\), write \(\omega_1^\downarrow\ge\cdots\ge\omega_q^\downarrow\) for the decreasing rearrangement of \((\omega_i)\), and define

\[ E_{\mathrm{end}}:= \sqrt{x_1x_q}\sqrt{\frac{1+x_1/\omega_1}{1+x_q/\omega_q}} \left[1+\frac{x_q+2\left(q-\sum_{i=1}^q x_i\right)}{\omega_q}\right]. \tag{3} \]

If

\[ \sum_{i=1}^q x_i<q, \qquad \sum_{s=m+1}^q \omega_s^\downarrow \ge q\left[\left(\frac qm\right)^\alpha-1\right], \qquad 1\le m<q. \tag{4} \]

then

\[ E_{\mathrm{end}} \prod_{i=1}^{q-1}K_{\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}}(x_i,x_{i+1}) \le C_\alpha, \tag{5} \]
Proof sketch

For a fixed schedule \(H\), sort the positive excesses \((h_k-1)_+\) as \(a_1\ge\cdots\ge a_r>0\), and define \[ B:=1+\sum_{k=1}^n\min\{h_k,1\}, \qquad D_s:=B+\sum_{\ell=s+1}^r a_\ell. \] When \(r\ge1\), choose \[ q=q(H,\alpha)\in \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{1\le s\le r}D_s\,s^\alpha. \] For the main case \(q\ge2\), apply Lemma 2 to the top \(q\) excesses, restored to chronological order, and call them \(c_1,\ldots,c_q\). Let \(b_i=S_i+1\), let \(v=S_{q+1}+1\), and put \(L:=D_q/q\). The unselected excesses lie in the intervening gaps, so \[ D_q=\sum_{i=1}^q b_i+v. \] Set \(x_i:=b_i/L\) and \(\omega_i:=c_i/L\). Then \(\sum_i x_i<q\), and the decreasing rearrangement satisfies \(\omega_s^\downarrow=a_s/L\). Minimality of \(q\) gives \[ 1+\frac1q\sum_{s=m+1}^q \omega_s^\downarrow =\frac{D_m}{D_q}\ge\left(\frac qm\right)^\alpha, \] which is exactly (4).

Write \(V_I\) for the selected-chain value in Lemma 2. Direct multiplication and telescoping of its neighboring factors gives \[ V_I^{-1} = L\,\widehat E_{\mathrm{end}} \prod_{i=1}^{q-1}K_{\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}}(x_i,x_{i+1}), \] where \[ \widehat E_{\mathrm{end}} = \sqrt{x_1x_q} \sqrt{\frac{1+x_1/\omega_1}{1+x_q/\omega_q}} \left[1+\frac{x_q+2(q-\sum_i x_i)-L^{-1}}{\omega_q}\right] \le E_{\mathrm{end}}. \] Thus (5) yields \(V_I\ge q/(C_\alpha D_q)\).

The clean problem is pointwise slightly stronger at finite scale because it drops the favorable term \(-L^{-1}/\omega_q\). It is nevertheless equivalent for the best uniform constant. Given any abstract data satisfying (4), take \(L\) large, use selected excesses \(L\omega_i\), pre-transition gap totals \(Lx_i-1\), and terminal gap total \(L(q-\sum_i x_i)-1\). Split every nonnegative gap total into steps of size at most one. This realizes the normalized data with \(r=q\) and \(D_q=Lq\). Moreover, for every \(m<q\), condition (4) gives \[ \frac{D_m}{D_q} = 1+\frac1q\sum_{s=m+1}^q \omega_s^\downarrow \ge\left(\frac qm\right)^\alpha, \] so this \(q\) is indeed a minimizing rank. Finally, \(\widehat E_{\mathrm{end}}\uparrow E_{\mathrm{end}}\) as \(L\to\infty\).

Eliminating the variables \(x_i\)

To make (5) more tractable, we first eliminate the variables \(x_i\). For a parameter \(\lambda>0\), to be chosen later, define \[ \Gamma_\lambda(w,z) := \sup_{x,y>0} \left\{ \log K_{w,z}(x,y)-\lambda(x+y) \right\}. \tag{6} \] By definition, this gives an upper bound for each edge kernel. \[ K_{w,z}(x,y) \le \exp\!\left(\lambda(x+y)+\Gamma_\lambda(w,z)\right). \] Multiplying these inequalities naturally produces the sum \(\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}(x_i+x_{i+1})\). Condition (4) gives \[ \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}(x_i+x_{i+1}) \le 2\sum_{i=1}^q x_i<2q. \] The proof sketch shows that the same exponential penalty also absorbs the endpoint factor \(E_{\mathrm{end}}\).

Lemma 4. Every sequence satisfying (4) obeys

\[ E_{\mathrm{end}} \prod_{i=1}^{q-1}K_{\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}}(x_i,x_{i+1}) \le C_{\alpha,\lambda} \exp\!\left\{ 2\lambda q+ \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}) \right\}. \tag{7} \]
Proof sketch

Applying the definition of \(\Gamma_\lambda\) to every edge gives the exponential of \[ \lambda\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}(x_i+x_{i+1}) + \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}). \] Since \[ \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}(x_i+x_{i+1}) = 2\sum_{i=1}^q x_i-x_1-x_q, \] it remains to bound \[ E_{\mathrm{end}} e^{-\lambda\left( 2(q-\sum_i x_i)+x_1+x_q \right)}. \] Condition (4) gives \(\omega_1,\omega_q\ge\alpha\). The last display is therefore a polynomial-growth function of the three nonnegative variables \(x_1,x_q,q-\sum_i x_i\), multiplied by exponential decay. Its supremum is a finite constant \(C_{\alpha,\lambda}\), which proves (7).

For completeness, the two-dimensional supremum in (6) can itself be reduced to one scalar. Its objective tends to \(-\infty\) when \(x\) or \(y\) approaches zero and when \(x+y\to\infty\). Together with the negative-definite Hessian computed in Lemma 5, this gives a unique maximizer. The first-order equations imply \[ x\left(1+\frac{x}{w}\right) = y\left(1+\frac{y}{z}\right) =:\tau. \] Define \[ X_w(\tau):=\frac{\sqrt{w^2+4w\tau}-w}{2}, \qquad A_w(\tau):=w+X_w(\tau). \] The maximizing pair is \(x=X_w(\tau)\), \(y=X_z(\tau)\), where \(\tau>0\) is the unique solution of \[ \frac1{2\tau} + \frac1{A_w(\tau)+A_z(\tau)} = \lambda. \] The left-hand side is strictly decreasing from \(+\infty\) to \(0\), so the solution exists and is unique. Its derivative is nonzero. The implicit-function theorem therefore makes the optimizer smooth in \((w,z)\), justifying the differentiation below.

Basic properties of \(\Gamma_\lambda\)

Lemma 5. For every \(\lambda>0\), \(\Gamma_\lambda\) has the following properties.

(1) Symmetry. \(\Gamma_\lambda(w,z)=\Gamma_\lambda(z,w)\).

(2) Joint convexity. The map \((w,z)\mapsto\Gamma_\lambda(w,z)\) is jointly convex on \((0,\infty)^2\).

(3) Decreasing in each coordinate. \(\Gamma_\lambda\) is strictly decreasing in each coordinate.

(4) Strict submodularity. \[ \partial_{wz}^2\Gamma_\lambda(w,z)<0. \tag{8} \]

Proof sketch

For fixed \(w,z\), write \(A=w+x\), \(B=z+y\), and \(\Sigma=A+B\). The Hessian of the objective in (6), in any nonzero direction \((a,b)\), is \[ -\frac{a^2}{2x^2}-\frac{b^2}{2y^2} -\frac{(a+b)^2}{\Sigma^2} +\frac{a^2}{2A^2}+\frac{b^2}{2B^2}<0. \] Hence the optimizer is unique. Symmetry is immediate.

For fixed \(x,y\), the Hessian in \((w,z)\) has quadratic form \[ \frac{a^2}{2w^2}+\frac{a^2}{2A^2} +\frac{b^2}{2z^2}+\frac{b^2}{2B^2} -\frac{(a+b)^2}{(A+B)^2}\ge0. \] The first four terms dominate \(a^2/A^2+b^2/B^2\), which dominates the last term by Cauchy--Schwarz. Taking the supremum preserves convexity.

Danskin's theorem gives \[ \partial_w\Gamma_\lambda = \frac1\Sigma-\frac1{2w}-\frac1{2A}<0, \] and similarly in \(z\). Implicit differentiation of the scalar optimizer equation gives \[ \partial_z\tau<0,\qquad \partial_z\Sigma>0,\qquad \partial_z A_w(\tau(w,z))<0. \] Therefore \[ \partial_{wz}^2\Gamma_\lambda = -\frac{\partial_z\Sigma}{\Sigma^2} +\frac{\partial_z A_w(\tau(w,z))}{2A^2}<0. \]

Upper bounding \(\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1})\)

Recall from Lemma 3 that \(\omega_1^\downarrow\ge\cdots\ge\omega_q^\downarrow\) is the decreasing rearrangement of \(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_q\).

For a positive vector \(v=(v_1,\ldots,v_N)\) and \(0\le k\le\lfloor N/2\rfloor\), let \(\mathcal M_k(N)\) be the set of all collections of \(k\) disjoint unordered pairs from \([N]\). Define \[ \Phi_{k,\lambda}(v_1,\ldots,v_N) := \max_{M\in\mathcal M_k(N)} \sum_{\{a,b\}\in M}\Gamma_\lambda(v_a,v_b). \] Thus \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}(v)\) is the largest value of a \(k\)-edge matching formed from the entries of \(v\). We use the convention \(\Phi_{0,\lambda}=0\).

The odd and even edges of the chronological path form two matchings.

Set \(k_q:=\lfloor(q-1)/2\rfloor\). Comparing both with the same maximum matching gives \[ \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}) \le 2\Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\omega_q^\downarrow,\ldots,\omega_2^\downarrow) +C_{\alpha,\lambda}. \tag{9} \] By the coordinatewise-decreasing property of \(\Gamma_\lambda\) in Lemma 5, replacing a matched entry by an unused entry that is no larger can only increase the matching sum. When \(q\) is odd, each parity matching leaves one index unused. If it contains \(\omega_1^\downarrow\), make this replacement. When \(q\) is even, one parity matching is perfect. Remove its edge containing \(\omega_1^\downarrow\). In the other matching, replace \(\omega_1^\downarrow\), if present, by an unused entry that is no larger. Condition (4) implies \(\omega_q^\downarrow\ge\alpha\), hence every \(\omega_i\ge\alpha\). The removed edge is therefore bounded by a constant depending only on \(\alpha\) and \(\lambda\).

We now bound \(\Phi_{k_q,\lambda}\) through two finer reductions, using the properties of \(\Gamma_\lambda\) established in Lemma 5.

Step 1. Replace \((\omega_q^\downarrow,\ldots,\omega_2^\downarrow)\) by \((\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)})\)

For \(2\le s\le q\), define the explicit comparison weights

\[ \bar w_s^{(q)} := q^{1+\alpha}\bigl((s-1)^{-\alpha}-s^{-\alpha}\bigr), \qquad 2\le s\le q. \tag{10} \]

Their tails satisfy \[ \sum_{s=m+1}^q\bar w_s^{(q)} =q\left[\left(\frac qm\right)^\alpha-1\right]. \] Together with (4), this gives \[ \sum_{s=m+1}^q\omega_s^\downarrow \ge \sum_{s=m+1}^q\bar w_s^{(q)}, \qquad 1\le m<q. \] Because \(\Gamma_\lambda\) is symmetric, jointly convex, and coordinatewise decreasing (Lemma 5), the maximum-matching functional \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}\) is symmetric, convex, and coordinatewise decreasing as well. The tail comparison above therefore gives \[ \Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\omega_q^\downarrow,\ldots,\omega_2^\downarrow) \le \Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)}). \] The proof below gives the majorization argument.

Step 2. Reduce to the worst pairing

Let \(0<v_1\le\cdots\le v_N\) and \(0\le k\le\lfloor N/2\rfloor\). Since \(\Gamma_\lambda\) decreases in each coordinate, a maximizing matching in \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}(v)\) may be chosen to use the \(2k\) smallest entries. Strict submodularity then pairs the two ends of this ordered list. \[ \Phi_{k,\lambda}(v_1,\ldots,v_N) = \sum_{j=1}^k \Gamma_\lambda(v_j,v_{2k+1-j}). \tag{11} \] The numbers in (10) satisfy \(\bar w_q^{(q)}\le\cdots\le\bar w_2^{(q)}\), so (11) applies directly to \((\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)})\).

It remains to estimate this explicit sum. By the mean value theorem, for every \(2\le s\le q\), there is \(\xi_s\in(s-1,s)\) such that

\[ \bar w_s^{(q)}=W_\alpha(\xi_s/q), \qquad W_\alpha(t):=\alpha t^{-1-\alpha}. \tag{12} \]

Using (12), the paired sum in (11) is a Riemann sum for the integral below. The proof controls the endpoint and rounding errors uniformly in \(q\) and gives

\[ \Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)}) \le q\int_0^{1/2} \Gamma_\lambda\bigl(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(1-t)\bigr)\,dt +C_{\alpha,\lambda}. \]

Combining this estimate with (9) gives the following lemma.

Lemma 6. Fix \(\alpha,\lambda>0\). There is a constant \(C_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty\) such that, for every integer \(q\ge2\) and every positive sequence \(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_q\) satisfying the tail condition in (4),

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{q-1}\Gamma_\lambda(\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}) \le 2q\int_0^{1/2} \Gamma_\lambda\bigl(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(1-t)\bigr)\,dt +C_{\alpha,\lambda}. \tag{13} \]
Proof sketch

We first prove (9). Split the temporal path into odd and even edges and put \(k_q=\lfloor(q-1)/2\rfloor\). If \(q\) is odd, each matching leaves one coordinate unused. Whenever a matching contains \(\omega_1^\downarrow\), replace it by an unused coordinate that is no larger. If \(q\) is even, remove the edge containing \(\omega_1^\downarrow\) from the perfect matching. In the other matching, make the same replacement if necessary. Coordinatewise decrease can only increase the resulting sums. Taking \(m=q-1\) in (4) gives \[ \omega_q^\downarrow \ge q\left[\left(\frac q{q-1}\right)^\alpha-1\right] \ge q\alpha\log\frac q{q-1}\ge\alpha, \] where the last step uses \(\log(1+t)\ge t/(1+t)\). Hence the removed edge is at most \(\Gamma_\lambda(\alpha,\alpha)\), which proves (9).

Each fixed-matching sum in the definition of \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}\) is convex and coordinatewise decreasing. Taking the maximum over all matchings preserves these properties. The collection of matchings is invariant under coordinate permutations, so \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}\) is also symmetric.

Let \(a_1\le\cdots\le a_N\) and \(b_1\le\cdots\le b_N\) satisfy \(\sum_{i=1}^\ell a_i\ge\sum_{i=1}^\ell b_i\) for every \(\ell\). Choose an ordered subgradient \(g\in\partial\Phi_{k,\lambda}(a)\) with \(g_1\le\cdots\le g_N\le0\). Symmetry and convexity give the ordering, while coordinatewise decrease gives the signs. Put \(D_\ell:=\sum_{i=1}^\ell(b_i-a_i)\le0\). Summation by parts yields \[ g\cdot(b-a) =g_ND_N+\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}(g_\ell-g_{\ell+1})D_\ell\ge0. \] The subgradient inequality therefore gives \(\Phi_{k,\lambda}(a)\le\Phi_{k,\lambda}(b)\), which is the weak-supermajorization comparison used above.

The reference entries also satisfy \[ \bar w_s^{(q)} =q^{1+\alpha}\int_{s-1}^{s}\alpha u^{-1-\alpha}\,du. \] Since the integrand decreases, this proves \(\bar w_q^{(q)}\le\cdots\le\bar w_2^{(q)}\). Their tail sums telescope to the right-hand side of (4), so the preceding majorization argument proves the comparison in part A.

For \(a\le b\le c\le d\), submodularity gives \[ \Gamma_\lambda(a,d)+\Gamma_\lambda(b,c) \ge\Gamma_\lambda(a,c)+\Gamma_\lambda(b,d), \] and \[ \Gamma_\lambda(a,d)+\Gamma_\lambda(b,c) \ge\Gamma_\lambda(a,b)+\Gamma_\lambda(c,d). \] Coordinatewise decrease selects the \(2k\) smallest coordinates, and repeated applications of these two exchange inequalities produce the pairing in (11).

Write \(q=2r+1\) or \(q=2r\). The paired sum produced by (11) becomes, respectively, \[ \sum_{j=1}^{r} \Gamma_\lambda\!\left(\bar w_{j+1}^{(q)},\bar w_{q+1-j}^{(q)}\right), \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{r-1} \Gamma_\lambda\!\left(\bar w_{j+2}^{(q)},\bar w_{q+1-j}^{(q)}\right). \] By the mean value theorem, for each \(2\le s\le q\) there is \(\xi_s\in(s-1,s)\) such that \[ \bar w_s^{(q)}=W_\alpha(\xi_s/q). \] Each paired sum is therefore a Riemann sum along \((t,1-t)\). We now justify a uniform endpoint estimate for \(G(t,s):=\Gamma_\lambda(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(s))\). Let \(w=W_\alpha(t)\), \(z=W_\alpha(s)\), and let \((x,y)\) be the optimizer in (6). Set \(A=w+x\), \(B=z+y\), and \(\Sigma=A+B\). The derivative formula in Lemma 5 gives the exact identity \[ -\partial_w\Gamma_\lambda =\frac1{2w}+\frac1{2A}-\frac1\Sigma =\frac{x}{2wA}+\frac{B}{A\Sigma}. \] For \(s\in[1/2,1]\), the value \(z\) stays in a compact subinterval of \((0,\infty)\). The scalar equation in Lemma 4 and \(A_z(\tau)\ge\sqrt{z\tau}\) give \[ \lambda \le \frac1{2\tau}+\frac1{\sqrt{z\tau}}, \] so \(\tau=O_{\alpha,\lambda}(1)\). Since \(x,y\le\tau\), both \(x\) and \(B=z+y\) are uniformly bounded. As \(A,\Sigma\ge w\), the preceding identity proves \[ -\partial_w\Gamma_\lambda=O_{\alpha,\lambda}(w^{-2}). \] Moreover \(|W_\alpha'(t)|W_\alpha(t)^{-2}=O_\alpha(t^\alpha)\). The derivative of \(G\) in its second coordinate is uniformly bounded because \(W_\alpha(s)\) and \(W_\alpha'(s)\) remain in compact ranges. Thus \(G\) has a bounded Lipschitz extension on \([0,1/2]\times[1/2,1]\). The constants are locally uniform when \(\alpha\) ranges over a compact subset of \((0,\infty)\).

It follows that, in either parity case, \[ \Phi_{k_q,\lambda} (\bar w_q^{(q)},\ldots,\bar w_2^{(q)}) =q\int_0^{1/2} \Gamma_\lambda\bigl(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(1-t)\bigr)\,dt +O_{\alpha,\lambda}(1). \] The possible central edge and the case \(q=2\) are absorbed into the same constant. Combining this estimate with (9) proves (13).

Finally, it remains only to verify a scalar inequality. Define

\[ J(\alpha,\lambda) := 2\lambda+ 2\int_0^{1/2} \Gamma_\lambda\bigl(W_\alpha(t),W_\alpha(1-t)\bigr)\,dt. \tag{14} \]

Combining Lemmas 4 and 6 gives

\[ E_{\mathrm{end}} \prod_{i=1}^{q-1}K_{\omega_i,\omega_{i+1}}(x_i,x_{i+1}) \le C_{\alpha,\lambda}e^{qJ(\alpha,\lambda)}. \tag{15} \]

Therefore, if \(J(\alpha,\lambda)\le0\), then (5) holds uniformly in \(q\), and Lemma 3 gives \[ R_n(H)\ge c_{\alpha,\lambda}(n+1)^{-(1+\alpha)}. \tag{16} \] The proof has thus become a scalar feasibility problem. It remains to find a small \(\alpha>0\) and some \(\lambda>0\) for which \(J(\alpha,\lambda)\le0\).

Take \(\alpha=0.6342\) and \(\lambda=0.4506\). A validated interval-arithmetic computation (verification appendix) certifies \[ J(0.6342,0.4506)<-0.00005<0. \] Hence \(R_n(H)=\Omega(n^{-1.6342})\), proving Theorem 2.

For reference, let \(\alpha_0\) be the unique zero of \(J(\alpha,0.4506)\), and define \(p_0:=1+\alpha_0\). Numerically, \(p_0\approx1.6341605846\).

Proof sketch

The boundary ranks are immediate. If there is no long step (\(r=0\)), the empty-chain Huber member of the same hard-function family gives \(R_n(H)\ge 1/(2B)\). If \(r\ge1\) and the minimizing rank is \(q=1\), that empty-chain witness together with the singleton instance of Lemma 2 gives \(R_n(H)\ge1/(4D_1)\). Since \(B\le n+1\), \(r+1\le n+1\), and \(D_1\le Br^\alpha\), both cases satisfy (16) after adjusting the universal constant.

Equation (15) and Lemma 3 show that, for the minimizing rank \(q\ge2\), \[ R_n(H)\ge \frac{q}{C_{\alpha,\lambda}D_q} e^{-qJ(\alpha,\lambda)}. \] If \(J(\alpha,\lambda)\le0\), the exponential factor is at least one. Minimality of \(q\) gives \[ D_qq^\alpha\le D_rr^\alpha=Br^\alpha. \] Hence \[ \frac q{D_q} \ge \frac{q^{1+\alpha}}{Br^\alpha} \ge \frac1{B(r+1)^\alpha}. \] Finally \(B\le n+1\) and \(r+1\le n+1\), proving (16).

For every fixed \(\lambda>0\), the map \(\alpha\mapsto J(\alpha,\lambda)\) is continuous and strictly decreasing. Indeed \(W_\alpha(t)\) increases with \(\alpha\), while \(\Gamma_\lambda\) decreases in each coordinate. The endpoint estimates in the proof of Lemma 6 give locally uniform domination.

As \(\alpha\downarrow0\), both profile coordinates tend to zero uniformly on, for example, \([1/4,3/4]\). Evaluating the supremum defining \(\Gamma_\lambda\) at any fixed \(x,y>0\) shows that its integrand tends uniformly to \(+\infty\) on \([1/4,1/2]\). Monotonicity supplies an integrable lower bound on the remaining part of the interval, so this divergence cannot be cancelled. Hence \[ \lim_{\alpha\downarrow0}J(\alpha,\lambda)=+\infty. \]

As \(\alpha\to\infty\), both profile coordinates are at least \(\alpha\). At the optimizer, \[ \Gamma_\lambda(w,z) \le \log\frac{x+y}{\alpha}-\lambda(x+y) \le-\log(\alpha\lambda)-1, \] uniformly in \(t\). Therefore \(\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}J(\alpha,\lambda)=-\infty\). Thus \(J(\,\cdot\,,0.4506)\) has a unique zero \(\alpha_0\).

Computer-assisted verification. For the exact decimal parameters \(\alpha=0.6342\) and \(\lambda=0.4506\), the validated computation partitions \([0,1/2]\) into \(2^{16}\) rational cells. On every cell it encloses the unique optimizing \(\tau\) by sign-verified brackets and evaluates \(\Gamma_\lambda\) using directed outward rounding. Concluding Remarks

Starting from the same hard-function construction and the corresponding chain bound in Lemma 2 (cf. Tsai's Lemma 6), we use a finer term-by-term analysis of the full chronological sequence. This improves Tsai's lower bound from \(\Omega(n^{-\sqrt3})\) to \(\Omega(n^{-1.6342})\).

At the exponential scale relevant to the final rate, our sequence estimates appear fairly tight, although we do not prove optimality. One plausible source of slack is the parity split in (9). The odd and even edges form two alternating matchings of the same chronological path, but we bound them independently by the same unconstrained worst-matching value. For strictly ordered weights, they generally cannot both realize the outside-in maximizer exactly.

A more structural question is whether Lemma 2 can already yield the \(\Omega(n^{-1.2716})\) lower bound, matching the silver-stepsize upper bound. Our refined analysis makes us skeptical that this route will close the whole gap, but it does not give an impossibility result. It may instead be necessary to construct hard functions with an explicit hierarchy of scales, mirroring the recursive structure of silver stepsizes, so that long steps at different scales interact within the same instance.
